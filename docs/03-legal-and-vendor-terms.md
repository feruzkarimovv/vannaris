# 03 — Legal, Vendor Terms, and Naming Clearance

**This document is directional research, not legal advice.** All ToS/AUP text below was extracted via direct fetch of each vendor's live legal pages on 2026-07-30. The extraction tool paraphrases and quotes selectively rather than returning raw HTML, so treat exact wording as close paraphrase with quoted fragments, not a certified legal transcript. Section numbers are as reported at fetch time and can change without notice. **Before launching publicly, have a real attorney review the specific benchmark methodology (what gets stored, what gets published, whether BYOK actually insulates SearchBench) — this is the single highest-value dollar spent before public launch, and this document should not be treated as a substitute for it.**

## The single most important finding in this document

Nearly every vendor's terms of service contain at least one of three things: a no-competing-product clause broad enough to arguably cover a routing SDK that helps developers substitute one vendor for another; a no-store/no-cache/no-republish clause that a public benchmark's methodology (storing query-response pairs, publishing scored comparisons) would technically implicate; or, in two vendors' cases, an explicit, unambiguous prohibition on benchmarking or competitive analysis without prior written consent.

**Seltz's** terms bar using its platform "for the purpose of benchmarking, performance testing, or competitive analysis, or publicly disclos[ing] the results of any such activities, without Seltz's prior written consent" — a direct, on-point restriction against exactly what SearchBench proposes to do. **Search Router's** terms (§10.3) bar using the service "to build or benchmark a competing service, except to the extent such restriction is prohibited by law" — the second vendor with an explicit no-benchmark clause. Beyond these two, **Brave's** ToS §2(b)(xvi) bars using its content "to create, train, evaluate, or improve...services" — arguably the single broadest anti-benchmarking restriction surveyed, since "evaluate" would literally cover a benchmark, even though it isn't labeled as one. **Tavily's** ToS §3.2(x) separately bars disclosing to any third party "any performance information or analysis" relating to the services — also squarely on point.

This means two of the nine vendors in scope contractually prohibit exactly what SearchBench intends to do, absent written consent, and at least two more have language broad enough to sweep it in. A cease-and-desist or account termination is a real, foreseeable risk if SearchBench queries these APIs at scale, stores results, and publishes comparative scores without first securing consent. The BYOK architecture (user brings their own API key; SearchBench's router calls the vendor using the end user's own credentials) is the correct and necessary mitigation for the resale/sublicensing clauses every vendor has, but **it does not, by itself, cure the no-benchmarking and no-competing-product clauses** — those attach to "use of the services" generally, and a benchmark that runs standardized queries and publishes a comparative score is a use of the services for benchmarking/competitive analysis regardless of whose API key is used.

**Practical recommendation for v1**: scope the initial public benchmark to vendors that do not have an explicit no-benchmarking clause (Exa, Perplexity, You.com, Serper, Linkup — none of these were found to have one, though see the per-vendor caveats below, especially Serper and Linkup where extraction was thin), while separately reaching out to Tavily and Brave for written consent or at minimum awareness before including them (both have broad-but-not-explicitly-benchmark-labeled clauses, and both, notably, run public self-benchmarks themselves — real leverage in that conversation). Do not include Seltz or Search Router in any published comparison without written consent given their explicit, unambiguous contractual bar. Reduce exposure further by publishing only derived numeric scores rather than republishing raw retrieved content or snippets, which reduces both copyright and ToS-storage exposure simultaneously.

## Legal precedent — is there any lawsuit history here?

No lawsuit, DMCA notice, or publicized cease-and-desist specifically targeting a comparative benchmark of search/retrieval APIs was found despite substantial searching. This is the strongest available evidence that the "don't trust any comparison, including ours" disclaimers some vendors publish are a defensive-marketing hedge rather than a response to real litigation history in this specific niche. This is a negative finding from real search effort, not proof that no risk exists — treat it as "no evidence found," not "no risk."

The closest and most relevant precedent is **Google LLC v. SerpApi, LLC** (N.D. Cal., filed 2025-12-19). Google alleged SerpApi circumvented Google's anti-scraping technology ("SearchGuard") in violation of DMCA §1201, and separately alleged breach of Google's own ToS via over 11 billion automated queries scraped and resold to third parties including Perplexity AI. SerpApi moved to dismiss in February 2026. On **2026-07-27** — three days before this research was conducted — a court dismissed Google's DMCA §1201 claims to the extent they covered ordinary, non-copyrighted search results, holding that DMCA anti-circumvention only protects technological measures guarding copyrighted works. The breach-of-contract claims were not the subject of this specific ruling.

This precedent is instructive but distinguishable in a way that matters a lot for SearchBench: SerpApi's exposure arises from unauthorized scraping of Google's own product — a company with no commercial API relationship to SerpApi, using anti-bot circumvention. SearchBench proposes to be a paying/free-tier customer calling each vendor's own published, authorized API under that vendor's own terms — no CFAA "unauthorized access" theory is available against an authorized API customer. That distinction does not eliminate the separate, real risk under each vendor's contractual no-benchmarking/no-competing-product clauses identified above — that exposure is a breach-of-contract/account-termination risk, unaffected by the SerpApi ruling either way. Notably, Linkup explicitly markets itself as the "safe alternative" to SerpApi-style vendors for exactly this reason, which is evidence the ecosystem is already actively thinking about this liability boundary.

**Vendor-run precedent worth citing back if challenged**: Tavily itself maintains a public, MIT-licensed GitHub repo (`tavily-ai/tavily-search-evals`) that benchmarks Tavily against Exa, Brave, Serper/Google, and Perplexity and publishes self-favorable results. A vendor whose own ToS bars others from "disclosing performance information or analysis" runs and publishes exactly that analysis itself when the result favors it. This doesn't resolve SearchBench's legal risk (Tavily benchmarking itself under its own IP is different from a third party benchmarking Tavily under Tavily's ToS), but it's a strong rhetorical and negotiating point, and evidence the ecosystem already normalizes this kind of comparison.

## Naming and trademark clearance

"SearchBench" has real, if not fatal, conflicts. `github.com/Talc-AI/search-bench` is an actual existing project, hyphenated but near-identical in name, that benchmarks consumer AI search chat products (Andi Search, You.com, Google Gemini, ChatGPT, Perplexity) via LLM-as-judge scoring, last updated 2024-08-30 and apparently dormant since. No license file was found and there's no live hosted leaderboard, just a static README table — low legal/trademark enforcement risk (no evidence of a registered mark or active commercial use) but real SEO/confusion risk and an awkward "someone already did a thing called SearchBench" precedent a journalist or competitor could raise. A Drupal.org module also named "SearchBench" (created 2008, unmaintained, different domain — CMS site-search internals) and a Hugging Face dataset named "SearchBench" (CS-algorithms search benchmarking, unrelated domain) are lower-risk, different-enough prior uses.

Domain status for `searchbench.com`/`.ai`/`.dev`/`.io` could not be conclusively determined this session — tool results were inconsistent across repeated attempts (conflicting DNS-resolution and robots.txt signals), and direct WHOIS-site queries returned no usable lookup data. **This needs a manual registrar check (GoDaddy, Namecheap, or similar) before any commitment to the name.** Likewise, USPTO's TESS/Trademark Center is an interactive tool that could not be queried programmatically in this research; no evidence of an existing "SEARCHBENCH" registration surfaced in general web search, but this is not equivalent to a real clearance search.

Two candidate alternative names were checked and should be **rejected outright** as live, current conflicts: **"SearchArena"** collides with LMArena's actual, currently-promoted "Search Arena" product — a live leaderboard, a public GitHub repo, an ICLR 2026 paper, and an active X presence. **"QueryBench"** collides with `querybench.com`, a live product benchmarking database query engines (h2o, ClickBench, TPC-H), explicitly positioned (like SearchBench itself) against vendor-biased benchmarks — different vertical, but the name and mission are close enough to be confusing. "FetchBench" is available in the sense that no commercial conflict exists, but it's already used twice in unrelated academic CS contexts (a CPU-prefetcher security benchmark and a robotics-fetching benchmark), which adds search-noise without adding risk.

The cleanest candidate found this session, with no conflicting product, company, repository, or trademark signal in any search performed, is **SearchRef** (or spelled out, "Retrieval Referee" — which directly evokes the "neutral referee for search APIs" framing). This is the recommended fallback if a zero-conflict name matters more than the (moderate, not severe) risk of proceeding with "SearchBench."

**Bottom line on naming**: usable but not clean if proceeding with "SearchBench" — do a real registrar WHOIS check and a real USPTO TESS search (not general web search) before filing anything or buying a domain, and go in with eyes open that a "SearchBench" already exists in a dormant, adjacent-enough GitHub project. SearchRef is the clean alternative if the founder would rather not deal with that friction.

## Open verification gaps (explicit, not guessed around)

Domain registration status for the SearchBench name family is unresolved and needs a manual check. USPTO federal trademark clearance was not performed programmatically and needs a real TESS search or a paid clearance search before filing. Linkup's full terms-of-use text did not fully load during this research (client-rendered placeholder); the resale and competing-purpose clauses reported came from secondary search-result fragments, not a direct read of the primary document — its output/training/storage clause specifically is unverified. Serper's full ToS extraction was thin; the absence of explicit competing-product or benchmarking clauses may reflect incomplete extraction rather than genuine absence, and should be manually re-read before relying on "Serper has no such clause" as a green light. No vendor in this set was confirmed to have a public, self-serve affiliate/reseller program; whether informal partnership or consent conversations are realistically available to a solo, unfunded founder is unverified and would require direct outreach. Finally, the absence of any lawsuit/DMCA/cease-and-desist specifically over a comparative search-API benchmark is a negative research finding, not a certified legal opinion — it should inform risk appetite, not replace an actual attorney's review before public launch.
