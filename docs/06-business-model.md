# 06 — Business Model

## The constraint that shapes everything here

`03-legal-and-vendor-terms.md` establishes that every vendor's terms of service bars reselling, sublicensing, or proxying access to their API. That forecloses the simplest possible monetization model (buy vendor access wholesale, mark it up, resell to developers) entirely. Every viable path here is BYOK: the developer holds their own vendor relationship and API keys; SearchBench monetizes the routing/orchestration decision layered on top, never the underlying query itself. This is not a limitation unique to SearchBench — it's exactly the model every comparable BYOK-orchestration company below has already been forced into, which is useful, because it means there are real, working pricing precedents to copy rather than needing to invent one from scratch.

Across the eight vendors with disclosed pricing, basic single-call queries cluster tightly at $0.005-0.008/query, with Serper a clear outlier cheap and Perplexity's Sonar (especially Deep Research mode) a clear outlier expensive — roughly a 15-20x spread between cheapest and priciest per-query cost.

**Measured, 2026-07-31, first full run** (150 queries × 5 cleared vendors). On the basis this benchmark is actually billed at, the spread is **23.3×** — Serper at $0.000300/query against Exa at $0.007000/query — and Serper reaches 90% or better of the leading score in four of the six categories. The table-price estimate above was directionally right and understated the gap.

**Corrected 2026-08-07: that 23.3× is not a like-for-like comparison, and this paragraph used to claim it was billed "at list".** Serper's $0.000300 is its top volume tier ($0.30/1,000), which requires a volume commitment; its published pay-as-you-go rate is $0.001/query. Compared like for like on pay-as-you-go the spread is **7.0×** against Exa, and **8.0×** across all five cleared vendors (Perplexity at $0.008 against Serper at $0.001). Both bases are published rather than chosen — `basis_per_query_usd` and `payg_per_query_usd`, per vendor, in `site/export/pricing.json`.

Which figure the pricing model should rest on depends on the customer, and the answer happens to be favourable: **23.3× is the spread available to a customer whose volume qualifies for Serper's committed tier, and that is precisely the customer the metered and savings-share tiers below are aimed at. 7× is what a small account sees.** The economic argument survives at both ends — it is a measurement rather than a projection either way — but it has to be quoted with the tier attached, because a reader who checks Serper's public pricing page will find $1/1,000 and not $0.30/1,000, and will be right to discount everything around it.

**The cost argument is also the only one this run supports.** `build_routing_gain` measures a per-category oracle — every category sent to its own best vendor — against a single vendor used for everything. The gain is **0.000 points**, because Exa has the highest score in all six categories, so the oracle is identical to hardcoding Exa. On this week's data the router is not worth anything as a *quality*-routing product. What it is worth is cost (a vendor at 7–23× less money holding 90%+ of the leading score in four of six categories) and reliability (`01-product-spec.md`, "What people renew for monthly is reliability, not scores"). Recomputable from `routing` in `site/data/latest.json`; the figure is published on the site rather than living only here.

## Pricing-model precedent (BYOK-orchestration and AI-gateway comparables)

Two clean archetypes recur across every comparable company surveyed, and neither charges a resale markup on the underlying provider's per-token/per-request cost as its primary model — consistent with the BYOK constraint SearchBench is also bound by.

**Flat usage-based metering, no subscription**: OpenRouter charges roughly a 5% fee on top of routed inference spend (5.5% with an $0.80 minimum on card top-ups specifically; 5% on crypto top-ups; no markup on the per-token inference rate itself; the first 1,000,000 BYOK requests/month are free, then 5% of the equivalent OpenRouter-hosted price). OpenRouter's growth trajectory under this model is the strongest available proof-of-concept for the whole category: revenue run-rate reportedly grew from roughly $400K/month (May 2025) to ~$19M annualized (end of 2025) to ~$50M annualized (March 2026), on total funding of $40.5M since a 2023 founding (seed $12.5M led by a16z, Series A $28M led by Menlo Ventures at a $500M valuation) — figures come from a paid research aggregator (Sacra), not OpenRouter's own disclosure, and should be treated as widely-reported-and-plausible rather than confirmed. Not Diamond charges a flat $0.05 per million tokens routed, pay-as-you-go, no free tier. Martian charges free for the first 2,500 requests, then $20 per 5,000 requests (an effective $0.004/request).

**Freemium log-volume SaaS subscription**: Portkey's tiers run free (10,000 logs/month) to $49/month (100,000 logs/month, then $9 per additional 100,000 up to 3M) to custom enterprise; Portkey raised a $15M Series A led by Elevation Capital (~Feb 2026). Helicone's tiers run free (10,000 requests/month) to $79/month (Pro) to $799/month (Team, 5 orgs) to custom enterprise — its disclosed funding figures ($5M seed at a $25M valuation, plus an earlier $125K pre-seed) rest only on funding-aggregator sites, not a Helicone press release, and should be treated as unverified.

## Recommended pricing shape — revised 2026-08-04

**What changed and why.** The previous version of this section recommended flat subscription tiers: $29-49/month for 50-100K routing decisions, $199-299/month for 500K-1M. That was modelled on Portkey and Helicone, and it has one structural flaw that the first real run made concrete. A flat cap prices by *logs stored*, which is a cost proxy, while the value delivered scales with *spend routed*. Those diverge violently here: the first run measured a **23.3× per-query cost spread** between the cheapest and most expensive cleared vendor at the tiers this benchmark is billed at (Serper at $0.0003/query on its committed volume tier, Exa at $0.0070/query), narrowing to **7.0×** if the same customer pays Serper's pay-as-you-go $0.001 — with Serper reaching 90% of the leading score in four of six categories either way. (Corrected 2026-08-07: this sentence previously said the figures were "measured not quoted". Exa and Perplexity return a billed cost on the call itself; Serper, You.com and Linkup do not, and their per-query figures are quoted from published price lists. `reported_by_vendor` in `site/export/pricing.json` says which is which, per vendor.) A customer routing ten million queries a month across that spread has five figures a month at stake. Charging them $299 is not a discount, it is a failure to participate in the value created — and it is worst precisely for the customers who benefit most, which is exactly backwards.

The revised model prices on volume, with a savings-share option where the savings are genuinely attributable.

| Tier | Price | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Free** | $0, no card | ~1,000 routing decisions/month, full open-source client, weekly score snapshot (not the live feed) |
| **Metered** | $0.001/decision, falling to $0.0007 above 500K and $0.0004 above 5M | Live score feed, hosted classifier, spend caps, query log retention |
| **Private eval** | $2,000-5,000 per run | The benchmark on the customer's own queries: scored table, recommended routing policy, modelled spend under it. The first thing sold, and the thing nothing free can substitute for |
| **Managed routing** | Floor + a share of measured savings, capped | For customers above roughly $5K/month of vendor spend, where a private eval has established a baseline to measure against |

**Why per-decision metering is the default self-serve tier.** It scales with usage rather than capping on the heaviest users, it is trivially comparable to what a developer already pays (Martian's effective $0.004/request; OpenRouter's ~5% take rate, which on a ~$0.007 blended vendor query cost is roughly $0.00035/query), and — the reason it should stay the default even as other tiers appear — it pays the same whichever vendor is chosen. A pricing model that is neutral by construction is worth a great deal to a company whose product is neutrality (`13-conflict-of-interest.md`).

**The savings-share tier, and its honest cost.** Charging a percentage of measured spend reduction aligns revenue with the cost spread the benchmark exists to surface (23.3× at committed volume, 7.0× pay-as-you-go — see the correction above), and it is the only model where a customer saving $40K/month pays proportionately. It also creates the one conflict of interest in this document that cannot be mitigated away: under it, Vannaris earns more when traffic moves to cheaper vendors, while also publishing the ranking that says which vendors are good enough to move to. That conflict is disclosed in `13-conflict-of-interest.md`, must be stated on the pricing page itself rather than only in a policy document, and is the reason the metered tier — not this one — should remain the default. Mechanically it needs: a baseline established by a private eval before routing starts, a floor so small accounts still pay something, a cap so the price cannot exceed what the metered tier would have cost, and a measurement window both sides agree on in advance. Without all four it is unsellable, and with them it is still the tier to revisit if the disclosure ever starts feeling uncomfortable to write.

**What is deliberately not priced.** The open-source client itself, forever. It is the distribution channel, and the thing being sold is the feed that keeps it current, the classifier that makes its routing accurate, and the evals that tell a customer what their own traffic is worth. Free open-source routers already exist; the winning position is to be the one everyone installs, not to be a paid alternative to one.

## Cost to run SearchBench itself

**Vendor API spend** (the benchmark's own query cost, running the harness against 200 queries × 8.5 vendors weekly): roughly **$47-94/month**, driven mostly by how many queries get routed through the pricier modes (Perplexity's high-context/Deep Research tiers, Exa's Deep Search). **Measured at the actual v1 scale** (150 public queries × 5 cleared vendors): **$3.38 per run**, about **$14.60/month** — well under the estimate, because the estimate assumed 8.5 vendors including the two most expensive and none of the queries use Deep Research modes. Adding the 30-question withheld set (`04-benchmark-methodology.md`) takes it to roughly **$4.06 per run, $17.60/month**. The estimate above is left in place as the planning figure for a larger vendor set; the measured figure is what the operation actually costs today. **LLM-judge grading** (per `04-benchmark-methodology.md`'s 3-judge cross-family ensemble design): estimated at roughly **$150-250/month**, and **the estimate is wrong by roughly an order of magnitude — corrected 2026-08-07.** The published run's judging consumed 3.61M prompt tokens and 143K output tokens across the three judges (Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, GPT-5.4 Mini), all of them small models; the session that measured it recorded **$2.07 per run, about $9/month**, against $150-250. The estimate assumed frontier judges rather than the cheap-model ensemble that was actually built. Two caveats on the $2.07: unlike vendor spend it is **not persisted per run** — `raw_responses.cost_usd` and `cost_source` record what each vendor call cost, and `judge_scores` records only token counts — so the figure is not reproducible from the database the way $3.38 is. Persisting judge cost alongside the tokens is the fix, and until it lands the token counts above are the reproducible part. **Compute/hosting**: effectively **$0/month** at the leanest (GitHub Actions on a public repo is free and unlimited for the weekly runner; Supabase and Vercel free tiers cover early dashboard traffic) up to roughly **$55-100/month** once the dashboard has real traffic and warrants Supabase Pro ($25/month) plus Vercel Pro ($20/user/month) plus a few dollars of object storage for data exports.

| Cost line | Lean/bootstrap | Comfortable/production | **Measured, v1 scale (2026-08-07)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vendor API spend | ~$50/mo | ~$100-150/mo | **$3.38/run ≈ $14.60/mo** (5 vendors, 150 queries) |
| LLM-judge grading | ~$10-25/mo (single cheap judge, no redundancy) | ~$50-150/mo (full 3-judge ensemble) | **$2.07/run ≈ $9/mo** (full 3-judge ensemble, cheap models) |
| Compute/hosting/DB/dashboard | ~$0/mo (all free tiers + GitHub Actions) | ~$55-100/mo | **$0/mo** (GitHub Actions + Vercel static, no build step) |
| **Total** | **~$75-125/month** | **~$225-400/month** | **~$5.45/run ≈ $24/month** |

**The measured column was added 2026-08-07 and the two estimate columns are left standing**, because the gap between them is the useful part: the real operation costs about a fifth of the lean estimate and a tenth of the comfortable one. The estimates assumed 8.5 vendors, frontier judges, and paid Supabase/Vercel tiers; the built system runs 5 vendors, three cheap judges, and static hosting. This matters beyond bookkeeping — at $24/month the track record accrues on a personal card regardless of any funding outcome, which is a materially different risk position from one where the benchmark stops if a cheque does not arrive.

This is consistent with a solo founder's stated resources (personal Mac plus rented cloud compute, no dedicated infra budget) — running the entire benchmark operation is a low-hundreds-of-dollars-per-month cost structure even at the comfortable end — and a $24/month one as actually built — not a capital-intensive undertaking, and well within what an f.inc first check ($100K-$250K) would cover for a very long runway. The two biggest cost-control levers are using GitHub Actions on a public repo instead of an always-on VM for the weekly runner, and choosing a cheap judge model for routine grading (reserving a pricier frontier judge only for spot-checks or disputed gradings).

## Open gaps in this analysis

Serper's exact live tier table could not be pulled directly from its own pricing page (returned a 404 on fetch) and was corroborated instead via a dated third-party tracker plus Serper's own homepage headline numbers — re-verify against Serper's live pricing page before treating the intermediate tier breakdown as vendor-confirmed. Search Router's actual pricing is undisclosed anywhere found beyond "2,000 free requests," and whether it's a single-vendor product or a multi-vendor aggregator is genuinely ambiguous between how prior research characterized it and how its own launch press release describes itself ("integrates with existing web-search services") — that press coverage is one wire release syndicated across five outlets, not five independent confirmations, and is worth a direct follow-up against Search Router's own site/docs. Helicone's and OpenRouter's funding/revenue figures rest on funding-aggregator sites or a paid research aggregator rather than each company's own primary disclosure — treat as plausible-but-unconfirmed. The overall unit-economics sketch above is a first-principles estimate built from verified per-unit prices, not an observed real-world bill from anyone actually running a benchmark at this exact scale — validate it empirically in the first month of real runs rather than trusting it as a guarantee.
