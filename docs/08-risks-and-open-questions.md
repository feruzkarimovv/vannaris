# 08 — Risks and Open Questions

This document exists so that nothing uncomfortable gets buried inside the more upbeat sections of the other files. It collects every real risk, every UNVERIFIED claim, and an honest run of this idea against the kill bar it was tested against before being recommended.

## The kill bar this idea was tested against

An idea gets killed if: it's already served well by an existing product, a feature a natural owner would absorb within a year, built on a false premise, unbuildable under the founder's actual constraints (solo, Apple Silicon Mac, no proprietary access, no audience, no network), or has no path to either revenue or a room like Founders Inc/YC. SearchBench was tested against this bar across two prior research rounds and this gap-closing pass, and survived — but "survived" means "not obviously dead," not "guaranteed to work." The honest risks below are what "not obviously dead" is actually resting on.

## Risk 1: the anti-benchmarking clauses are a real, live legal exposure, not a formality

This is the single most important update from this research pass. Two vendors in the target set (Seltz, Search Router) have terms of service that explicitly and unambiguously prohibit using their platform for benchmarking or competitive analysis, or publicly disclosing the results of such activity, without prior written consent. Two more (Brave, Tavily) have broad language — "evaluate...services," "disclos[e]...performance information or analysis" — that a reasonable reading could extend to cover exactly what SearchBench does. No lawsuit or DMCA action specifically targeting a comparative search-API benchmark was found in this research, which is real evidence the risk hasn't materialized for anyone else yet — but it is a negative finding from search effort, not a legal opinion, and it should not be mistaken for a green light. The mitigation plan in `03-legal-and-vendor-terms.md` (scope v1 to vendors without an explicit clause, seek written consent from the borderline cases, publish only derived scores rather than raw content, get an actual attorney to review the specific methodology before public launch) is a real plan, not a workaround that makes the risk disappear. A vendor could still send a cease-and-desist, terminate API access, or worse. The founder should go into this with clear eyes that this is a live, not hypothetical, exposure, and should budget real money for a lawyer's review before launch rather than treating this document as a substitute for one.

## Risk 2: the naming problem is unresolved

"SearchBench" collides, at least in name and rough category, with a dormant but real GitHub project. The domain registration status for the obvious TLDs could not be conclusively determined in this research. USPTO trademark clearance was not performed. None of this is fatal on its own, but it means the founder is currently building under a name that hasn't been cleared, and every week of building and publicly using "SearchBench" before that clearance happens is a week of switching-cost accumulating if the name turns out to need to change. Do the registrar WHOIS check and a real USPTO TESS search in the first week of building, not after months of using the name in public — it's cheap to check now and expensive to discover late.

## Risk 3: this market is getting noisier and more crowded, fast

Two new, real, funded competitors (Seltz, $12.5M seed; Search Router) appeared in the roughly four months between the prior research round and this one. Both are single-vendor products, not the neutral-benchmark-plus-router combination SearchBench is building, but both now publish self-run benchmarks as marketing — bringing the count of vendors doing this to at least five (Tavily, Seltz, Search Router, Quercle, fastCRW). This validates that the market wants proof-with-numbers, which is good, but it also means SearchBench has to be visibly, verifiably more neutral than an increasingly noisy field of lookalike claims, and has less time to establish that reputation before the category gets crowded enough that "yet another benchmark" stops getting attention on its own. Speed of execution and speed of building genuine public trust (not just shipping features) both matter more because of this than they did in the prior research round.

## Risk 4: LiteLLM could plausibly build the missing layer itself

LiteLLM has already shipped the "unified access across search vendors" layer that used to be the more commoditizable half of this opportunity. It has not shipped quality-based routing, and there's no public signal it's planning to — but LiteLLM is an active, well-resourced open-source project, and if it (or a well-funded fork) decided to add benchmark-informed routing before SearchBench has established itself, the wedge narrows substantially. This is a reason to move quickly and to seriously pursue the "build on top of LiteLLM's schema" integration strategy in `05-architecture.md` — becoming the routing-intelligence layer LiteLLM's own ecosystem plugs into is a stronger position than racing LiteLLM to build the same thing independently.

## Risk 5: the honest comparison to Speko.ai's trajectory has real limits

Speko.ai (the direct inspiration for this mechanism, benchmarking voice AI models) is a confirmed Y Combinator Summer 2026 company. That's a real, concrete, near-term bar — not a hypothetical one — and it's worth being honest about what would and wouldn't transfer. Speko had a first-mover position in its category before any competitor was publishing self-benchmarks; SearchBench does not have that same clean first-mover position, since at least five vendors in its category already publish self-serving comparisons, meaning SearchBench has to win on demonstrated neutrality rather than on being the first to have the idea at all. YC's acceptance rate is roughly 1-2% of applicants across any given batch, and reaching that bar requires the accumulated track record described in `07-build-plan.md`, not just a good idea — plenty of reasonable ideas with real execution don't get in, and that should be treated as a realistic possible outcome, not a remote edge case. Getting into f.inc or YC is also not the finish line even if it happens — it's a shot at the next round of proving the business actually works, and the honest range of outcomes after admission includes flame-out as much as it includes success.

## The honest bottom line

Nothing in this gap-closing research surfaced a reason to kill SearchBench outright. What it surfaced is a sharper, more specific picture of exactly what has to go right: the legal mitigation plan actually needs to hold up (get the lawyer review), the naming question needs to get resolved early rather than accumulating switching cost, execution needs to be fast because the field is getting noisier, and the founder should walk in with realistic odds on the accelerator outcomes rather than treating admission as a foregone conclusion. This is a buildable, defensible, honestly-recommended project — not a guaranteed one.
