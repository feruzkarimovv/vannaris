The finding

The cheapest API is not worse everywhere. It is worse in specific places.

How it works

Four steps, all of them inspectable.

Every step is in the public repository, and every intermediate result is in the export.

  1. The same queries, to every vendor

    queries across categories, sent to all APIs and normalised to the same top- depth. Without that, the most verbose vendor wins on volume.

  2. Three judges, three model families

    Each response is scored by one Anthropic, one OpenAI and one Google model against a published rubric. LLM judges prefer text that reads like their own; one family would bake that preference into every number.

  3. Median, and only when complete

    The published score is the median of the three. Responses missing any judge are dropped rather than averaged over what is left — a partly-computed number in the same column as a complete one is worse than a missing one.

  4. Everything published, including the holes

    Individual judge scores, per-response latency and cost, and the coverage of every cell ship as CSV. You can recompute every number on this site from the export.

Prior work

Other people have compared these APIs. Here is what is different.

At least five parties publish head-to-head numbers for these vendors. Every one of them either wins its own benchmark or cannot be recomputed from what it published.

The most substantial is tavily-ai/tavily-search-evals — a real, open, MIT-licensed evaluation harness scoring Tavily against Perplexity, Serper, Brave and Exa. It is decent work. It is also run by the vendor that wins it, run once rather than on a schedule, judged without a disclosed ensemble, and shipped without the per-judge scores that would let anyone re-derive the ranking. Seltz, Search Router, Quercle and fastCRW each publish a comparison in which they place first.

None of that makes them dishonest. It makes them unfalsifiable, which is a different problem and the one this exists to fix.

  • ✓ Multi-judge, with the disagreement published. models from labs score every response, and the share that split them by more than three points — — is on the methodology page, per category.
  • ✓ Open data, not just an open method. Every individual judge score, latency and cost exports under CC BY 4.0. Recompute the table and publish a different one if the numbers say so.
  • ✓ Questions nobody can tune against. A withheld set runs alongside the public one, its hash committed before it runs and its questions published when it retires.
  • ✓ No vendor pays anything. The conflicts that do exist are written down rather than denied.
What this funds itself with In development

The benchmark is free. The eval on your own traffic is not.

These questions are not your questions. The ranking that decides what you should actually call is the one your own production queries produce.

  1. Your queries, this harness

    Send a sample of your real traffic. It runs against every vendor here under the same rubric and the same three-judge ensemble, and comes back as a scored table on your queries, a routing policy, and what that policy would cost per month against what you spend now. Nothing about your traffic is published, ever.

  2. The client is free and open source

    Bring your own vendor keys. Failover when a vendor rate-limits or goes down, per-vendor timeouts, spend caps, caching with freshness-aware TTLs, and a query log — the layer you would otherwise write by hand. It never resells, proxies or marks up vendor access, and it works offline against a downloaded snapshot of these scores.

  3. The live feed is the paid part

    Fresh scores every cycle, the query classifier, and the vendor-reliability series behind them. Free routers already exist; the client is not the thing worth charging for, and keeping it current is.

  4. Priced so it cannot bend the benchmark

    Metered per routing decision, which pays the same whichever vendor is chosen. Where pricing could create an incentive to favour a vendor, it is disclosed rather than argued away.

Limitations

Known limits

What the data does not support, with the measurements behind each one.

The judges disagree, measurably

per response
Mean disagreement points, and a -point gap between judge families on the same responses. Per-family means.

The scores cluster high

every scored query
Almost everything lands between 7 and 10, which suggests the query set is not hard enough to discriminate cleanly at the top. Harder queries are the next change to it.
Absolute scores are soft, and so is the bottom of the ranking
An earlier version of this page claimed the ranking survived the spread above because every vendor faces every judge. That was wrong, and the export published the data that disproves it: judge bias is not a constant offset per judge, it interacts with the vendor. What actually holds — drop any one judge family and the top of places are unchanged. Score with a single family alone and even first place moves: of families put a different vendor on top. A claim like "scores 8.4 out of 10" is softer still.
No human calibration
The methodology calls for a human-labelled gold set scored monthly against the ensemble. It is not built. Until it is, the judges are unaudited.
Two vendors out of scope
Tavily and Brave are excluded: their terms of service are broad enough to cover third-party benchmarking, and consent has not been sought. Vendor scope.
Single region, single run
All queries were issued from one location at one time. Search results vary by region and by hour, and neither is measured.
